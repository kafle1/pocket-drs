\title{Bounce-Anchored Monocular Reconstruction of Cricket Deliveries: Leg-Before-Wicket Review from a
Single Phone Clip with Propagated Uncertainty}

\author{Niraj~Kafle%
\thanks{N. Kafle is an independent researcher in Kathmandu, Nepal (e-mail: contact.me.kafle@gmail.com).}}

\markboth{Submitted to IEEE Transactions on Instrumentation and Measurement}{Kafle: Bounce-Anchored Monocular Reconstruction}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Ball-tracking review in cricket uses six or more calibrated high-speed cameras, so club, school and net
cricket go without it. A single phone cannot triangulate, and a monocular projectile fit gets depth only from
the curvature gravity puts on the image track, a weak cue when the ball flies nearly along the optical axis.
We anchor the fit on the bounce. The contact instant is the split at which two parabolas best explain the
track; its pixel back-projects onto the calibrated ground plane to a metric point, and the remaining
parameters of both parabolas are then linear in the pixels. A nine-parameter two-parabola model with a free
post-contact horizontal velocity and an estimated restitution is refined by least squares, and its covariance
is propagated to the stump-plane crossing, where a guard band turns marginal Law 36 verdicts into umpire's
call. All accuracy figures are from simulation. On \GeoTotal{} synthetic deliveries with exact truth,
swept over noise, frame rate, calibration error, unmodelled aerodynamics and camera placement, the verdict agrees with the truth in \GeoAgreeAll\% of cases (answering not out every time would
score \GeoAlwaysNotOutPct\%), \GeoOutCalledOut{} of \GeoOut{} true outs are called out, \GeoFalseOut{} of
\GeoNotOut{} not-outs are called out, and median stump-plane errors are \GeoMedYAll~cm laterally and
\GeoMedZAll~cm vertically. A single parabola fitted across the whole track, bounce included, scores
\GeoAgreeAllParabola\% with medians of \GeoMedYAllParabola{} and \GeoMedZAllParabola~cm. On \EteN{} clips
rendered without drag or swing, the full pipeline agrees on \EteAgree{} with no false out. The fitted
covariance misses model error and covers \CovBFitYOne\% and \CovBFitZOne\% of errors at one sigma; a model
term cross-validated on the same simulator raises this to \CovBFullYOne\% and \CovBFullZOne\%. Three real
clips have no metric truth, and on two of them the stated sigma is smaller than the spread between repeat
analyses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} leg-before-wicket law is the most contested adjudication in cricket: an umpire must
judge in real time whether a ball that struck the batter's pad would have gone on to hit the stumps.
Broadcast decision review systems answer that counterfactual by triangulating the ball from six or more
synchronised, calibrated high-speed cameras and extrapolating the post-impact
path~\cite{owens2003hawkeye}. They are accurate, and confined to televised cricket. Club,
school and age-group matches, and every net session, ask the same questions with no instrument at all.

This paper asks how far a single phone can go. One camera cannot triangulate and should not claim the
accuracy of a rig. A cricket delivery, though, is a heavily constrained scene.
The ball is a sphere of known radius flying ballistically over a plane of known geometry, past two rigid
regulation-height targets that are always in view, and a pitched delivery touches that plane exactly once. Each of those
facts is a constraint, and together they make the monocular problem well posed whenever the bounce is seen
and a few frames follow it. We write this up
as a measurement paper, with a calibration from reference artefacts, an estimator with a propagated
uncertainty, a test of how well that uncertainty covers the true error, and a decision rule that uses it.

In the terms of instrumentation and measurement, the instrument is a consumer camera of unknown intrinsics,
the reference artefacts are the stumps and creases whose dimensions the Laws of Cricket fix, the measurand is
the ball's position where its projected path crosses the stump plane, and the output is a statement of
conformity against a limit the Laws set, carrying a band that accounts for the uncertainty of the measurement
itself~\cite{carullo2024conformity}. We have not found a reported instrument that combines one uncalibrated
hand-held camera, an uncertainty propagated to a measurand the camera never observes, and an acceptance band
widened by that uncertainty. Table~\ref{tab:soa} places the method among the closest systems.

Monocular projectile estimation~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles} recovers scale from gravity, a cue that is
weak when the ball travels along the optical axis, as it does from the natural phone position behind either
set of stumps; the apparent size of the ball~\cite{vanzandycke2022ball3d} is a second cue whose depth error
grows with the square of range. Section~\ref{sec:geometry} quantifies both.

We use the bounce as an anchor. The instant of contact shows up in the image as
the split at which two parabolas explain the track far better than one. At that instant the ball's centre is
one radius above a plane whose position calibration gives, so the contact pixel back-projects to a
metric point without any depth cue from the image. With the contact fixed, the velocities before and after it are linear
in the pixel measurements, and a nine-parameter model of the whole delivery can be initialised in closed form
and refined jointly. The paper makes three contributions. We formulate delivery reconstruction as a bounce-anchored
two-parabola fit with a free post-contact horizontal velocity, so seam and spin deviation off the pitch is
estimated where enough of the post-contact arc is seen, and an estimated restitution coefficient bounded to the
physical range (Section~\ref{sec:method}). We propagate the covariance of the fit to the stump-plane crossing
and widen the umpire's-call band of the Laws by it, so the system can decline a marginal call
(Section~\ref{sec:decision}), and we measure how well that uncertainty covers the true error. We evaluate
against exact truth from a simulator whose physics is richer than the estimator's, across noise, frame rate,
calibration error, dropped frames and six camera placements, against a single parabola on identical
detections, then through the full pipeline on rendered clips, and check repeatability on real hand-held
footage (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The system is meant for coaching and training review, not officiating, and it fails in known places: a
yorker leaves too few post-contact frames, a camera at the bowler's end sees the decisive arc foreshortened,
and on two of three real clips the stated sigma is too small. No result here is checked against an
independent measurement of a real ball. The system began as the author's undergraduate final-year project
at Phoenix College of Management, Lincoln University College; the method, evaluation and results reported
here are new.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Vision-based measurement}

We treat the phone as a measuring instrument in the sense of Shirmohammadi and
Ferrero~\cite{shirmohammadi2014camera}. Others have measured this way on consumer hardware,
recovering six degrees of freedom of a moving rigid body from smartphone
cameras~\cite{zhu2022smartphone6dof} and calibrating a mobile camera from scene structure and the onboard
accelerometer instead of a printed target~\cite{arik2022mobilecalib}.
We also skip the printed target and calibrate from the pitch itself, with no inertial sensor.

Ball trajectories have been measured with calibrated high-speed stereo rigs for table
tennis~\cite{zhang2010tabletennis} and golf~\cite{wang2019golf}. Single-view measurement of a moving target is
reported for moving points~\cite{huang2025monotraj}, for high-speed objects with a steered mirror or a second
moving platform~\cite{wang2024quasi1d,guo2026pose}, for tennis ball speed and spin from one
camera~\cite{zhu2026tennis}, and for the ball-racket rebound of a table-tennis robot~\cite{sun2024rebound},
whose velocity-dependent restitution is the closest analogue here to the bounce we anchor on. Each fixes scale with a second view, a steered
or known range, or a controlled setting, and none is a hand-held phone looking down twenty metres of pitch at
a ball a few pixels wide.

The uncertainty of the result is the other half of this literature: covariance through stereo
triangulation~\cite{dileo2011covariance}, analytic propagation for convergent
stereo~\cite{ezebili2024uncertainty}, and binocular uncertainty from a first-order linearised
covariance~\cite{he2026binocular}, the device used here. Where a learned component sits in the chain, the
guidance is to quantify what it contributes~\cite{alosman2021mluq,buchicchio2025uq}, so we report end-to-end
results with the detector in the loop separately, and we sweep the extrinsic calibration error whose effect
on monocular measurement is studied in~\cite{wang2024extrinsicerror}. Here the propagated sigma also sets the
width of a decision band, as measurement uncertainty does in conformity assessment~\cite{carullo2024conformity}.

\subsection{Ball tracking from one view}

Ribnick et al.~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles} show that a projectile's position and velocity can be estimated
from a single view by exploiting the known gravitational acceleration, in a formulation where a parabola's six
parameters appear linearly once the projection equations are cross-multiplied; our pre- and post-contact
solves are of that form with three unknowns instead of six, because the anchor supplies the position. Van
Zandycke and De Vleeschouwer~\cite{vanzandycke2022ball3d} regress ball depth from its apparent size in a
calibrated image, which we use only in the fallback for a delivery whose contact is not observed. Closest to
our setting, Ghimire et al.~\cite{ghimire2026cricket} estimate a cricket ball's trajectory from one calibrated
camera: an initial depth from image-plane measurements is smoothed by a Kalman filter under a ballistic model,
and the bounce is then detected on the filtered track. In their method the bounce comes out of the estimated
trajectory. In ours it is what makes the trajectory observable, and the verdict carries a propagated uncertainty.

\subsection{Cricket ball physics and estimation}

Mehta~\cite{mehta2005swing} reviews the aerodynamics of swing and Robinson and
Robinson~\cite{robinson2013projectile} give the equations of a spinning sphere under drag and lift, which
the synthetic generator integrates; Carr\'e et al.~\cite{carre2004cricketball} model the impact of a cricket
ball on a rigid surface and James et al.~\cite{james2004pitches} measure the bounce of real pitches, which
set the restitution range the estimator allows. The leg-before-wicket law is Law 36 of the MCC Laws~\cite{mcc2017laws}; the
umpire's-call bands of professional ball tracking are set out in the ICC playing
conditions~\cite{icc2023drs}.

\section{Geometry and Observability}
\label{sec:geometry}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\columnwidth]{geometry.pdf}
\caption{The model. A delivery is two gravity parabolas joined at the contact point, whose height above the
pitch is the ball radius. The phone sits behind one set of stumps, so the ball travels nearly along the
optical axis and its depth is the least observable coordinate.}
\label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

We use a right-handed pitch frame with origin at the striker's stumps: $x$ runs down the pitch toward the
bowler, $y$ across it, $z$ up (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}). A pinhole camera with intrinsics $K$ and pose
$(R, \mathbf{t})$ maps a world point $\mathbf{p}$ to camera coordinates $\mathbf{p}_c = R\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{t}$
and to the pixel $(u, v) = (f x_c / z_c + c_x,\ f y_c / z_c + c_y)$. The stumps are $h_s = 0.711$~m high, the bails reach $h_b = 0.724$~m, and
the outer edges of a three-stump set are $\pm w_o = 0.1143$~m from the middle stump; the pitch is
$L = 20.12$~m long and $3.05$~m wide; the ball has radius $R = 0.036$~m.

\subsection{Why one parabola is not enough}

Between release and contact the ball is a projectile,
$\mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{p}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0 t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 \hat{\mathbf{z}}$, with six unknowns.
Each frame gives two equations, and after cross-multiplying the depth out of the projection the system is
linear in the six unknowns~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles}. The absolute scale is fixed only by $g$: a
trajectory twice as far away and twice as fast projects to the same pixels at every instant except for the
gravitational sag, the one term that does not scale. Seen from behind the stumps the sag is a small
correction to a nearly linear image path, so the system is ill-conditioned along the depth-and-speed
direction.

The apparent size of the ball gives an independent depth $d = fR/r$ from its pixel radius $r$, but
$\partial d / \partial r = -d/r$, so an error $\delta r$ in the measured radius produces
$\delta d = d^2 \delta r / (fR)$: quadratic in range. At 15~m with $f = 950$~px the ball is 2.3~px in radius
and half a pixel of radius error is 3~m of depth. So the cue can start a fit but cannot carry one.

\subsection{What the contact supplies}

A pitched delivery touches the ground once. At the contact instant $t_b$ the centre of the ball is at
$(x_b, y_b, R)$: two unknowns on a known plane. If the contact pixel $(u_b, v_b)$ can be found, the ray
through it meets the plane $z = R$ at a unique metric point. No image depth cue is involved, since the calibration
already gives the plane. Given that anchor, the pre-contact trajectory
$\mathbf{p}(t) = (x_b, y_b, R) + \mathbf{v}^- \tau - \tfrac{1}{2} g \tau^2 \hat{\mathbf{z}}$, $\tau = t - t_b < 0$,
has three unknowns instead of six, and the post-contact trajectory with $\mathbf{v}^+$ likewise. Each side is
a linear system with two equations per frame and three unknowns, over-determined from two frames, and the
depth direction is no longer degenerate because the anchor pins the trajectory in metres at one instant and
$g$ pins the time scale. Section~\ref{sec:results} measures the difference as a factor of ten in error.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Calibration from the marks the Laws fix}

The user taps the four corners of each stump set, striker's end then bowler's, and the four pitch corners,
which lie at $(0, \pm w_o, \{0, h_s\})$, $(L, \pm w_o, \{0, h_s\})$,
$(0, \pm 1.525, 0)$ and $(L, \pm 1.525, 0)$. SQPnP~\cite{terzakis2020sqpnp} solves the pose over a sweep of focal length and, unless it is pinned, pitch
length from 2 to 25~m; the lowest-error pose is refined by least squares on the pixels. The stumps alone leave the focal-length-against-length trade-off nearly degenerate; the pitch corners, off the stump plane, break it, and are dropped only when they disagree with the stumps by more than
a frame-scaled tolerance, because the stump height is the one dimension the Laws fix exactly. A pose whose
reprojection error exceeds $\max(25, 0.03W)$~px for frame width $W$, whose camera height is outside 0.1 to
5~m, or whose fitted length is outside 2 to 25~m is rejected with a request to re-tap. The camera's end is inferred from which stump rectangle is larger in the image; with the batter's
handedness this fixes which sign of $y$ is the leg side.

\subsection{Detection and association}

Per frame, a YOLO detector~\cite{jocher2023ultralytics}, fine-tuned on photos of cricket, tennis and other
sports balls~\cite{kuznetsova2020open,lin2014coco} and on cricket video frames with YouTube-sourced frames
excluded~\cite{rfcricv2,rftracker}, and a classical detector combining hue thresholding with frame-difference motion each emit candidates with a
pixel centre, radius and confidence; a location detected in more than 30\% of frames over more than 60\% of
the clip's span is static clutter and is suppressed, as is any candidate too large or too small for a
ball anywhere in the flight volume. Candidates are associated into one track by RANSAC over
a constant-acceleration image model~\cite{fischler1981ransac}: seed pairs propose an arc, inliers within a
radius scaled to the frame diagonal are counted, the best arc is refined by weighted least squares, and a
second pass over the unclaimed detections stitches on the far side of a bounce when it continues the same
horizontal motion. An arc the flight model cannot fit to 12~px rms is discarded and the next best tried.
The learned track is preferred. A sustained reversal of horizontal image travel marks the bat or pad
interception and ends the live track. The output is a list of $(t_i, u_i, v_i, r_i, w_i)$: time, pixel
centre, pixel radius and a confidence used as a weight.

\subsection{Finding the contact and initialising}

For every split index $j$ from three frames in to three frames from the end, one quadratic in $t$ is fitted
to each pixel coordinate on each side and the total weighted residual is recorded, alongside that of a
single quadratic over the whole track. The best split is accepted as a contact if it explains the pixels at
least twice as well as the unsplit fit, which a full toss does not. The contact instant is where the two
fitted arcs meet, and the contact pixel is the mean of the two arcs there.

The contact pixel back-projects to $(x_b, y_b, R)$. Seen from far down the pitch a pixel of error on the
ground plane is metres, so four further candidates are tried, the ground crossings of six-parameter parabola fits
to each side and the back-projections of the two frames adjacent to the split, and the candidate whose
refined fit explains the pixels best is kept. For each candidate,
$\mathbf{v}^-$ and $\mathbf{v}^+$ come from the linear solves of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}, and the initial
restitution is $e_0 = -v_z^+ / v_z^-$ clipped to $[0.25, 0.85]$.

\subsection{Joint refinement}

The delivery is parameterised by $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (t_b, x_b, y_b,\allowbreak v_x^-, v_y^-, v_z^-,\allowbreak v_x^+, v_y^+, e)$,
with $v_z^+ = -e\, v_z^-$, so
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}(t) = (x_b, y_b, R) + \mathbf{v}^{\mp} \tau - \tfrac{1}{2} g \tau^2 \hat{\mathbf{z}}, \quad \tau = t - t_b,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
taking $\mathbf{v}^-$ for $\tau < 0$ and $\mathbf{v}^+$ for $\tau \geq 0$. The residual vector stacks the
weighted pixel residuals $w_i (u_i - \hat{u}_i)$ and $w_i (v_i - \hat{v}_i)$ of every frame with three soft
priors: $(e - 0.55)/0.15$, $(v_x^+ - v_x^-)/4$ and $(v_y^+ - v_y^-)/2$, in units chosen so that each prior
is worth about one pixel of disagreement. The post-contact horizontal velocity is free to differ from the
pre-contact one, which is how seam and spin deviation and friction loss are measured; when the post-contact
arc is only a few frames long the priors keep the problem determined and the covariance reports the
resulting uncertainty. The cost is minimised with a soft-L1
loss~\cite{triggs2000bundle} at a scale of 2~px by the bounded trust-region reflective
method~\cite{branch1999trf,virtanen2020scipy}, with $t_b$ bounded to the track's span, $e$ to
$[0.25, 0.85]$, and velocities to physical ranges. The pixel radii are not used: they are unreliable from a
box detector and the anchored model does not need them.

The covariance of the estimate is the linearised $\hat{\sigma}^2 (J^\top J)^{-1}$ with $J$ the Jacobian at
the solution and $\hat{\sigma}^2$ the residual variance per degree of freedom~\cite{seber1989nonlinear}. It
is a first-order approximation and Section~\ref{sec:coverage} measures how far it can be trusted.

\subsection{Deliveries without an observed contact}

A full toss, or a clip cut before the pitch, has no split. The six-parameter single parabola is then solved
linearly~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles}, refined with the apparent-size residual $w_i (r_i - fR/\hat{d}_i)$ at
weight 0.3, and expressed in the parameterisation of~\eqref{eq:model} by placing the unobserved contact where
the parabola would meet the ground with the prior restitution; the unobserved deviation and restitution carry
their prior spread in the covariance. The result is flagged and its uncertainty is large. This is also the estimator we compare against, since it is what a
monocular method without the contact constraint can do.

\subsection{Prediction and decision}
\label{sec:decision}

The post-contact parabola is continued to the stump plane $x = 0$; if it would touch down again first, a
further bounce with the same $e$ is applied. The crossing $(y^\star, z^\star)$ and its covariance follow
from~\eqref{eq:model} and a numerical Jacobian of the crossing with respect to $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, giving
$\sigma_y$ and $\sigma_z$. The impact point is the model position at the last tracked frame.

Law 36~\cite{mcc2017laws} is applied as three tests. The ball must not have pitched outside the line of the
leg stump; it must have struck the batter in line with the stumps (impact outside the line of off is not out
if a shot was offered, which we cannot see, so impact outside the line on either side is treated as not in line, to the batter's benefit); and it must be projected to hit the stumps, meaning $|y^\star| \leq w_o$ and
$z^\star \leq h_b$ for the centre. The playing conditions~\cite{icc2023drs} return umpire's call when the centre is outside a stump edge by
less than one ball radius $R$. We add a guard band on top of that rule~\cite{jcgm106,carullo2024conformity}: a
test is passed only if the centre is at least $k\sigma$ inside its limit, and failed only if it is more than
$R + k\sigma$ outside, with $\sigma$ the standard uncertainty of that coordinate; anything in between is
umpire's call. The app ships $k = 1$ on the fitted covariance, and every verdict in Experiments~1 and~2 uses
that rule; Section~\ref{sec:kcurve} shows it is too permissive and sets $k$ from the risk of a false out.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Changes since submission.} Three faults are fixed and every experiment was rerun. Frames carried the
requested time, not their own timestamp, which at 30~Hz cut agreement from \FpsThirtyFixedAtSixtyAgree\% to
\FpsThirtySubmittedAtSixtyAgree\%. The pose is now refined on the pixels, taking calibration's lateral spread
at the stumps from \CalUnrefSdY{} to \CalAppSdY~cm. The lost detector weights were retrained with cricket
video frames added, and the size and flight tests added after a photo-only retrain fired on a large round
object in one real clip.

\subsection{Synthetic deliveries with exact truth}

A generator integrates each delivery at 1~kHz from release to the stump plane. Its physics is deliberately
richer than the estimator's model: aerodynamic drag with $C_d = 0.5$ on a 0.16~kg ball; a lateral acceleration before contact
(swing~\cite{mehta2005swing}); a fractional loss of horizontal speed and an added lateral velocity at
contact (friction and spin off the pitch); and a restitution coefficient drawn per delivery from 0.40 to 0.70, the range
of real pitches~\cite{james2004pitches}. Release speed is drawn from 60 to 150~km/h, pitching length from 1 to
10~m, line from $-0.6$ to $0.6$~m, release height from 1.8 to 2.3~m. The batter's pad intercepts the ball at
the popping crease, 1.22~m from the stumps, so detections stop there and the estimator must predict the rest; the
true crossing is the integrated trajectory continued untouched, and the true verdict follows from the true
contact, crease and stump-plane points by the same Law 36 tests with $k = 0$.

Observations are the projections of the true positions at the frame rate, with Gaussian pixel noise on the
centre, 10\% multiplicative noise on the radius, a dropout probability per frame, and the calibration taps
perturbed by Gaussian noise before the pose is re-solved, so the estimator never receives the true camera; the focal length is solved from the taps with $L$ fixed.
The centre noise is independent per frame. The real detector's error has not been measured against
hand-marked centres, so we do not know which row of the noise sweep a real clip sits in.
The reference configuration is 60~Hz, 1~px centre noise, 5\% dropout, 2~px tap noise, all physical effects
on, and a phone 2.8~m behind the striker's stumps at 1.7~m height. Each factor is swept in turn over
\GeoPerCell{} random deliveries per setting, and six camera placements are tested at the reference noise:
three positions at the striker's end, a lateral offset of 1.5~m, and two placements at the bowler's end.
Of all deliveries \GeoNotOut{} are truly not out, \GeoOut{} out and \GeoUmp{} umpire's call, so always
saying not out would agree \GeoAlwaysNotOutPct\% of the time; we report false and missed outs as well. Both
estimators get identical detections, but the single parabola is fitted across the whole track, bounce
included, which it cannot represent, so its error mixes missing depth with model misfit.

\subsection{Rendered clips through the full pipeline}

To include detection and association error, the fixed 100-delivery grid of five speeds (90 to 145~km/h),
five lines ($0, \pm 0.2, \pm 0.4$~m) and four lengths (2, 4, 6 and 8~m) is rendered by our own renderer to 1080 by 1920 portrait
clips at 60~Hz with a flat pitch, stumps, creases and a motion-smeared ball, and each clip is submitted to the
production job with the calibration marks perturbed by \EteTapPx~px, exactly as the app submits it. The
learned detector was not trained on rendered frames, so this tests the geometry end to end rather than the
detector's robustness to clutter, which the real clips do.

\subsection{Real clips}

Three hand-held phone clips of net deliveries, an outdoor off-spinner with a white ball and two indoor
deliveries with a red ball, are analysed with their stump and corner taps. The instrument in all three is an
unmodified phone: a rolling-shutter CMOS sensor read out at 1080 by 1920 in portrait, 8-bit 4:2:0 chroma
subsampling, H.264 at 5 to 7~Mb/s, and a nominal 60~Hz frame interval that is not exact, one clip averaging
59.8~Hz. Subsampling and compression blur the ball's edge and move the detected centroid, which is the
pixel-noise term Experiment~1 sweeps from 0 to 5~px, and the frame-interval jitter enters through $t$
(Table~\ref{tab:budget}). In simulation, rolling-shutter skew up to 30~ms changes neither accuracy nor
coverage measurably (Section~\ref{sec:coverage}). No metric truth exists for these clips,
so the paper reports what needs none: the output with its stated uncertainty, and repeatability. Each clip is
analysed on all sampled frames, on the even frames only and on the odd frames only; the half-rate analyses
see disjoint detections of the same delivery, and the spread between their predictions is an empirical
precision to hold against the propagated sigma.

Verdict agreement is the fraction of deliveries whose returned class equals the true class. A false out is a
not-out delivery returned as out, the error a review system must not make; a missed out is the reverse.
Errors are absolute differences between the predicted and true stump-plane crossing in $y$ and $z$, and
between the estimated and true contact point; medians and 90th percentiles are reported because the
distributions are heavy-tailed. Coverage is the fraction of errors within one,
two and three propagated sigmas.

Table~\ref{tab:sweeps} gives the controlled result. At the reference configuration
the anchored estimator agrees with the true verdict in \RefAgree\% of deliveries with a median stump-plane
error of \RefMedY~cm laterally and \RefMedZ~cm vertically, and 90th percentiles of \RefPNineZeroY{} and
\RefPNineZeroZ~cm. The single parabola on the same detections agrees in \RefAgreeParabola\% with median errors of
\RefMedYParabola{} and \RefMedZParabola~cm. The factor of ten fits the observability argument of
Section~\ref{sec:geometry}, but it overstates what the anchor alone buys, because the baseline also fits one
parabola across a bounce. A lateral error of a quarter of a metre is a coin toss on a target 23~cm wide.

Across the sweeps the anchored estimator's agreement lies between \SweepAgreeMin\% and \SweepAgreeMax\%, and
the single parabola's between \SweepAgreeMinParabola\% and \SweepAgreeMaxParabola\%. Frame rate has almost no effect on agreement from 30 to 240~Hz (\FpsThirtyAgree\% at 30~Hz), because
the anchor is one well-determined event and the parabolas on either side are over-determined from a handful
of frames; the stated sigma, though, shrinks with the frame count while the model error does not. Calibration tap error has little effect across the range tested
(\TapEightAgree\% at 8~px, \TapTwelveAgree\% at 12~px), because twelve marks set the pose, and the stump plane rather than any single tap sets the anchor. Dropped frames to 40\% cost nothing measurable
(\DropFortyAgree\%). Pixel noise on the ball centre does cost agreement, which is \NoiseZeroAgree\% at zero
noise, \NoiseTwoAgree\% at 2~px, \NoiseThreeAgree\% at 3~px and \NoiseFiveAgree\% at 5~px, with the vertical
error rising from \NoiseZeroMedZ{} to \NoiseFiveMedZ~cm. The system responds as intended: the number of
umpire's calls rises from \NoiseZeroUmp{} to \NoiseFiveUmp{} while false outs stay at \SweepFalseOutMax{} or
fewer in every row. Unmodelled drag, swing, friction and spin together move agreement from \PhysOffAgree\% to \PhysOnAgree\%
and the median errors from \PhysOffMedY{} and \PhysOffMedZ~cm to \PhysOnMedY{} and \PhysOnMedZ~cm: the
constant-velocity model absorbs drag into an average speed over the short arc and the free post-contact
velocity absorbs friction and spin.

Pooling every anchored reconstruction, of \GeoNotOut{} deliveries that were not out \GeoFalseOut{} were
returned as out (\GeoFalseOutPct\%) against \GeoFalseOutParabola{} for the single parabola, of \GeoOut{} that
were out \GeoOutCalledOut{} were called out and \GeoMissedOut{} not out, and \GeoNoVerdict{} returned no
verdict. Nearly every other disagreement is a decisive class returned as umpire's call or the reverse. The
contact point is located to \GeoMedContactX~cm along the pitch and \GeoMedContactY~cm across it. The release
speed, an arc average, is off by a median \GeoMedSpeedErr~km/h, too coarse to report as a speed reading.

Placement matters less than which end the phone films from. Behind the striker's stumps, low, high or back,
agreement is \CamStrikerMinAgree\% to \CamStrikerMaxAgree\%, and \CamStrikerSideAgree\% with a 1.5~m offset
to the side. From the bowler's end it is \CamBowlerRefAgree\% and \CamBowlerSideAgree\%, with median vertical
errors of \CamBowlerRefMedZ{} and \CamBowlerSideMedZ~cm, because the decisive post-contact arc is 15 to 20~m
away and foreshortened. The app asks the user to film from behind the batter; the placement table is in the
supplement.

\subsection{Choosing the guard band from false-out risk}
\label{sec:kcurve}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{risk.pdf}
\caption{World~B. Left: largest specific false-out risk among OUT calls against $k$, with 1 and 5\,\%
limits. Right: agreement (dashed) and umpire's call (solid). Vertical lines: the chosen $k$.}
\label{fig:risk}
\end{figure}

Following JCGM~106~\cite{jcgm106}, the specific risk of an OUT call is the probability, given the estimate,
that the true verdict is not out, $1 - \prod_i \Phi((m_i + R_i)/\sigma_i)$ over the signed margins $m_i$
of the four limits (pitching line, impact line, stump edge, top of the bails), with $R_i = R$ except on the
pitching line and a flat prior. Fig.~\ref{fig:risk} traces it on the \RiskN{} World~B
deliveries, \RiskTruthOut{} of them truly out. The shipped rule, pipeline sigma and $k = 1$, makes out calls
whose risk under the full sigma reaches \RiskShippedMaxSpecFull\%, with \RiskShippedOutsOverFive{} above
5\,\%. With the full sigma every out call is below 5\,\% from $k = \RiskFullKFive$ and below 1\,\% from
$k = \RiskFullKOne$; the sample-free bounds with all four limits binding at once are \RiskBoundFourFive{} and
\RiskBoundFourOne. The price is agreement, \RiskFitKOneAgree\% as shipped against \RiskFullAgreeFive\%
and \RiskFullAgreeOne\%, and umpire's call on \RiskFullUmpFive\% and \RiskFullUmpOne\% of deliveries,
because \RiskFullOutToUmpFive\% of true outs come back as umpire's call at $k = \RiskFullKFive$. No setting
has a missed out. These risks use the full sigma, whose model term was fitted on the same simulator, so they
hold under its physics, not on a real pitch. We recommend the full sigma with $k = \RiskFullKFive$ for
coaching and $k = \RiskFullKOne$ for anything closer to officiating; the released app still uses the fitted
covariance alone.

\subsection{End to end on rendered clips}

Through the production job, with the learned detector, RANSAC association and the calibration re-solved
from perturbed taps, the pipeline agrees with the true verdict on \EteAgree{} of \EteN{} deliveries with
\EteFalseOut{} false outs and \EteMissedOut{} missed outs; every other disagreement is an umpire's call
(the per-length table is in the supplement). The median stump-plane error is \EteMedY~cm laterally and
\EteMedZ~cm vertically, the contact point is located to \EteMedContactX~cm along the pitch and
\EteMedContactY~cm across it, and the release speed to \EteMedSpeedErr~km/h. The stump-plane errors are below
Experiment~1's reference because the grid flies without drag, swing or spin, the setting in which
Experiment~1 gives \PhysOffMedY{} and \PhysOffMedZ~cm. The median stated sigma is \EteMedSigmaY~cm laterally and \EteMedSigmaZ~cm
vertically, and the system answers with umpire's call on \EteUmp{} deliveries. Only \EteNotOutTruth{} of
the deliveries are not out, so no false out among them says little about a rate below a few percent.

The disagreements have physical causes. Yorkers at 2~m agree in \EteLenTwoAgree\% of cases with
\EteLenTwoUmp{} umpire's calls, because the ball is intercepted 0.8~m after it pitches and the post-contact
arc holds one or two frames; at 4, 6 and 8~m agreement is \EteLenFourAgree, \EteLenSixAgree{} and
\EteLenEightAgree\%. On the middle-stump line, \EteMiddleOutToUmp{} of the \EteMiddleOutTruth{} deliveries that are truly out are
returned as umpire's call: the vertical band at the top of the stumps is where the
propagated sigma is widest, so the system declines to call it.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{No independent reference.} Every accuracy number, and the model term, comes from our own simulator
and renderer; the real clips show only within-clip repeatability, on one phone, one operator and three
deliveries. A second phone at right angles, or a commercial tracker, would supply a reference.

\textbf{Traceability stops at the Laws.} Scale comes from the nominal stump and pitch dimensions, not from
measured ones, and time from the phone's own clock, which is not checked against a reference.

\textbf{The baseline is weak.} The single parabola is fitted across the bounce; an unanchored two-parabola
fit would isolate what the anchor adds.

\textbf{The stated uncertainty misses the flight model.} Swing, drag and pitch variation, the largest
inputs of the budget, are outside the covariance, and the Monte Carlo interval is shifted as well as wider.
An ambiguous contact split makes the solution multimodal, which no Gaussian sigma represents.

\textbf{The estimator ignores drag, swing, friction and spin.} Swing with a side-force coefficient of 0.3
moves the median lateral error from \PhysBaselineMedY{} to \PhysSwingCsZeroThreeMedY~mm and biases the
height by \PhysSwingCsZeroThreeMeanZ~mm; a pitch with restitution 0.40 biases it by
\PhysRestitutionZeroFourZeroMeanZ~mm. The reported release speed is an arc average, not the speed
at the hand.

\textbf{The contact must be seen, and calibration is manual.} A full toss, or a hidden contact, falls back
to the flagged single-parabola fit. Twelve taps are required; detecting the marks would remove them.

\section{Conclusion}

In simulation, a single phone behind the batter can reconstruct a cricket delivery in metres if it uses the
bounce, which fixes a point on a known plane at a known instant. On \GeoTotal{} synthetic deliveries with
exact truth the anchored estimator makes \GeoFalseOut{} false outs among \GeoNotOut{} not-outs, with median
stump-plane errors of \GeoMedYAll{} and \GeoMedZAll~cm, against \GeoMedYAllParabola{} and
\GeoMedZAllParabola~cm for a single parabola fitted across the bounce; on rendered clips the full pipeline
agrees on \EteAgree{} of \EteN. The fitted uncertainty misses model error. With a model term and a guard band
set from the risk of a false out it can decline calls the evidence does not support, as umpire's call does,
but on two of three real clips it is still too small. It is a coaching aid whose accuracy on a real pitch is
not yet measured, and it does not replace a multi-camera rig.
