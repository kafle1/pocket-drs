\title{Measuring a Cricket Ball's Stump-Plane Crossing and Its Uncertainty from a Single Phone Clip:
Bounce-Anchored Reconstruction for Leg-Before-Wicket Review}

\author{Niraj~Kafle%
\thanks{N. Kafle is an independent researcher in Kathmandu, Nepal (e-mail: contact.me.kafle@gmail.com).}}

\markboth{Submitted to IEEE Transactions on Instrumentation and Measurement}{Kafle: Bounce-Anchored Monocular Reconstruction}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We measure where a cricket ball would cross the plane of the stumps, with a stated uncertainty, from one
phone clip. The application is leg-before-wicket review in club and net cricket, where
broadcast ball tracking from several calibrated high-speed cameras is not available. One phone cannot
triangulate, and gravity's curvature is a weak depth cue when the ball flies along the optical axis. We anchor the fit on the bounce: the contact pixel
back-projects onto the calibrated ground plane to a metric point, after which both parabolas are linear in
the pixels. All accuracy and coverage figures are simulated. The rule the app ships is built to avoid false
outs: on \RiskN{} simulated deliveries it calls \RiskCalFalseOutFive{} not-outs out, calls
\RiskCalOutRecallFive\% of true outs out and returns \RiskCalOutToUmpFive\% of them as umpire's call. Its
agreement with the true verdict, \RiskCalAgreeFive\%, shows little skill: always saying not out scores
\RiskAlwaysNotOutPct\% on the same deliveries. Its guard factor is chosen in sample; under independent
normal tests with $\sigma = w_{95}/1.96$ the modelled risk is bounded at 5\,\% for any delivery only from a
factor of \RiskBoundFourFive, and the held-out one-sided tails at that level run from \CalExceedMin\% to
\CalExceedMax\%. The uncalibrated estimator, scored on its own covariance with a guard factor of 1, has
median stump-plane errors of \GeoMedYAll{} and \GeoMedZAll~mm over \GeoBalls{} balls and \GeoSettings{}
error settings and agrees in \GeoAgreeAll\% of runs, against \GeoAlwaysNotOutPct\% for always saying not
out; on World~B its covariance covers \CovBFitYOne\% and \CovBFitZOne\% of errors at one sigma. Calibrated per camera
end on the \CalPooledN{} deliveries with a bounce fit (\CalExcluded{} excluded), the 95\,\% half-width covers
\CalYCovNinetyfive\% and \CalZCovNinetyfive\% of held-out errors, as split-conformal calibration ensures by
construction. On \EteN{} rendered clips from the striker's end the app agrees on \EteAgree{} (always not
out: \EteNotOutTruth) with no false out; its 95\,\% half-width covers \EteCoverNinetyfiveY\% of lateral
errors but \EteCoverZLenTwo{} of \EteCoverZLenTwoN{} height errors at 2~m; from the bowler's end it agrees on
\EteBowlerAgree{} of \EteBowlerN{} with \EteBowlerNoVerdict{} no verdict. The app declines \RealDeclinedRuns{} of the
\RealRuns{} real-clip analyses. The three real clips ran on a
pose from the stumps alone; on the two with the pitch length free, a mode the simulation never tests, it
fitted lengths of \RealTestFourLength{} and \RealTestFiveLength~m, and no real delivery has been
measured with a reference.

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{his} paper measures one quantity from one phone clip: the point where a delivered cricket
ball would cross the plane of the stumps, with its uncertainty. That point decides the leg-before-wicket law,
the most contested adjudication in cricket: an umpire must
judge in real time whether a ball that struck the batter's pad would have gone on to hit the stumps.
Broadcast ball-tracking systems such as Hawk-Eye, whose tennis system Owens et al.\ describe~\cite{owens2003hawkeye},
triangulate the ball from several synchronised, calibrated high-speed cameras, and in cricket extrapolate the post-impact path.
Their error is not shown with the call~\cite{collins2008hawkeye}, and they are confined to televised
cricket. A commercial app offers ball tracking and LBW review
from one phone~\cite{fulltrack2026}, but its website states no accuracy or uncertainty. Club, school and
age-group matches, and net sessions, ask the same questions without a characterised instrument.

One camera cannot triangulate, but a cricket delivery is a constrained scene: a ball of known radius flies
ballistically over a plane of known geometry, past stumps of regulation height, and touches the plane once.
In simulation these constraints make the scale observable whenever the bounce is seen.

The instrument is a consumer camera of unknown intrinsics, the reference artefacts are the stumps and
the pitch dimensions the Laws of Cricket fix, and the verdict is a statement of conformity against a limit the Laws
set, with a band for the measurement's own uncertainty~\cite{carullo2024conformity}. We have
not found a reported instrument that combines one uncalibrated phone camera, an uncertainty propagated
to a measurand the camera never observes, and an acceptance band widened by it.

Gravity~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles} and the ball's apparent size~\cite{vanzandycke2022ball3d} give depth
only weakly from behind the stumps (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). We use the bounce as an anchor: at contact
the ball's centre is one radius above the calibrated ground plane, so the contact pixel back-projects to a
metric point, and the velocities on either side become linear in the pixels. The paper makes three
contributions. We formulate delivery reconstruction as a bounce-anchored
two-parabola fit with a free post-contact horizontal velocity, so seam and spin deviation off the pitch is
estimated (median error \GeoMedDevErr~m/s in simulation, for a true lateral kick drawn from $-2$ to
2~m/s), and an estimated restitution coefficient bounded to
the physical range (Section~\ref{sec:method}). We propagate the fit's covariance to the stump-plane
crossing, calibrate it per camera end on held-out simulated data and widen the umpire's-call band by it
(Section~\ref{sec:decision}). We evaluate against exact simulated truth, against the same split without
the anchor, on rendered clips, and on three real clips (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The system is meant for coaching and training review, not officiating, and it fails in known places: a
yorker leaves too few post-contact frames, a camera at the bowler's end sees the decisive arc foreshortened,
and on the real clips the calibration from the stumps alone, a mode the simulation never tests, gives
implausible scale. No result here is checked against an independent measurement of a real ball. The system began as the author's undergraduate final-year project
at Phoenix College of Management, Lincoln University College; the method, evaluation and results reported
here are new.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Vision-based measurement}

We treat the phone as a measuring instrument in the sense of Shirmohammadi and
Ferrero~\cite{shirmohammadi2014camera}, as others have on smartphones~\cite{zhu2022smartphone6dof}, and
calibrate it from the pitch itself. Ball and object trajectories have been measured with stereo
rigs~\cite{zhang2010tabletennis}, from one view~\cite{huang2025monotraj}, with a steered
mirror~\cite{wang2024quasi1d}, from moving platforms~\cite{guo2026pose}, for tennis speed and
spin~\cite{zhu2026tennis}, and a ball-racket rebound model has been built for a table-tennis
robot~\cite{sun2024rebound}. These fix scale with a second
view, a known range or a controlled setting, not from a phone looking down twenty metres of pitch at a ball
a few pixels wide.

The uncertainty of such results has been propagated through stereo
triangulation~\cite{dileo2011covariance,ezebili2024uncertainty} and as a first-order linearised binocular
covariance~\cite{he2026binocular}, the tool we also use. Where a learned component sits in the chain, the
guidance is to quantify what it contributes~\cite{alosman2021mluq,buchicchio2025uq}, by a GUM
model~\cite{angrisani2025mlgum} or Monte Carlo~\cite{angrisani2026mlmodel}. We do neither for the detector
and report results with it in the loop separately; we sweep the extrinsic calibration error
of~\cite{wang2024extrinsicerror}.

\subsection{Ball tracking from one view}

Ribnick et al.~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles} estimate a projectile from one view using gravity, with a
parabola's parameters linear after cross-multiplying; our solves have that form with three unknowns. Van
Zandycke and De Vleeschouwer~\cite{vanzandycke2022ball3d} recover depth from the ball's known size, a cue we
use only when the contact is not observed. Monocular 3-D ball trajectories
have been recovered in badminton~\cite{liu2022monotrack}, tennis~\cite{ertner2024synthnet} and table tennis~\cite{kienzle2025spin}, by lifting
a 2-D track to 3-D~\cite{ponglertnapakorn2025ball} or by learning 3-D position from 2-D labels under the
laws of motion~\cite{kienzle2024physics}. TT3D~\cite{gossard2025tt3d} is the nearest in idea: it
reconstructs table-tennis flights from one broadcast camera by choosing the bounce state that minimises the
reprojection error. These works report accuracy; none that we found propagates an uncertainty to the
reconstructed point and tests its coverage. Dedicated 2-D ball trackers~\cite{tarashima2023wasb,raj2025tracknetv4} were not compared with our detector. Closest to
our setting, Ghimire et al.~\cite{ghimire2026cricket} estimate a cricket ball's trajectory from one calibrated
camera by Kalman filtering under a ballistic model and then detect the bounce on the filtered track. There
the bounce comes out of the trajectory; here it makes the trajectory observable.

The synthetic generator integrates the equations of a spinning sphere under drag and
lift~\cite{robinson2013projectile} with swing as reviewed by Mehta~\cite{mehta2005swing}. Law 36 of the MCC Laws~\cite{mcc2017laws} defines leg before wicket, and the ICC playing
conditions~\cite{icc2023drs} the umpire's-call bands.

\section{Geometry and Observability}
\label{sec:geometry}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\columnwidth]{geometry.pdf}
\caption{The model. A delivery is two gravity parabolas joined at the contact point, whose height above the
pitch is the ball radius. The phone sits behind one set of stumps, so the ball travels nearly along the
optical axis and its depth is the least observable coordinate.}
\label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

We use a right-handed pitch frame with origin at the striker's stumps: $x$ runs down the pitch toward the
bowler, $y$ across it, $z$ up (Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}). A pinhole camera with square pixels, focal length
$f$ in pixels, principal point $(c_x, c_y)$ and pose $(R_\mathrm{c}, \mathbf{t})$ maps a world point
$\mathbf{p}$ to camera coordinates $\mathbf{p}_c = R_\mathrm{c}\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{t}$
and to the pixel $(u, v) = (f x_c / z_c + c_x,\ f y_c / z_c + c_y)$. The stumps are $h_s = 0.711$~m high, the bails reach $h_b = 0.724$~m, and
the outer edges of a three-stump set are $\pm w_o = 0.1143$~m from the middle stump; the pitch is
$L = 20.12$~m long and $3.05$~m wide; the ball has radius $R = 0.036$~m.

Before the contact the ball is a projectile with six unknowns, linear in the pixels once the depth is
cross-multiplied out~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles}. Its scale is fixed only by $g$: a trajectory twice as
far and twice as fast projects to the same pixels except for the gravitational sag, a small correction seen
from behind the stumps, so the fit is ill-conditioned along depth and speed. The ball's pixel radius gives
depth $d = fR/r$ with error $d^2 \delta r / (fR)$: at 15~m and $f = 950$~px, half a pixel is 3~m. At the
contact instant $t_b$, however, the centre is at $(x_b, y_b, R)$, and the ray through the contact pixel
meets the plane $z = R$ at one metric point. Given that anchor each side has three unknown velocities, a
linear system over-determined from two frames, and the anchor pins metres while $g$ pins time.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Calibration from the marks the Laws fix}

The user taps the four corners of each stump set and the four pitch corners, which lie at
$(0, \pm w_o, \{0, h_s\})$, $(L, \pm w_o, \{0, h_s\})$, $(0, \pm 1.525, 0)$ and $(L, \pm 1.525, 0)$.
SQPnP~\cite{terzakis2020sqpnp} solves the pose over a sweep of focal length and, unless it is pinned, pitch
length from 2 to 25~m, and the best pose is refined by least squares on the pixels. The pitch corners, off
the stump plane, break the near-degenerate trade-off of focal length against length. An implausible pose is
rejected with a request to re-tap. The tap order, striker's stumps first, fixes the frame, and the
batter's handedness fixes which sign of $y$ is the leg side. Section~S0 of the supplement gives the tolerances.

\subsection{Detection and association}

Per frame, a YOLO detector~\cite{jocher2023ultralytics}, fine-tuned on ball photos~\cite{kuznetsova2020open,
lin2014coco} and cricket video frames~\cite{rfcricv2,rftracker}, and a classical colour and motion detector
emit candidates with a pixel centre, radius and confidence. Static clutter and candidates of impossible size
are suppressed, and the rest are associated into one track by RANSAC over a constant-acceleration image
model~\cite{fischler1981ransac}, with a second pass that stitches the far side of a bounce. A sustained
reversal of horizontal image travel marks the bat or pad and ends the track (details in Section~S0). The
output is a list of $(t_i, u_i, v_i, r_i, c_i)$: the frame's own decode timestamp, pixel centre, pixel
radius and a tracker confidence $c_i \in (0, 1]$, 0.4 times the detector score plus 0.6 times the
tightness of the track fit; residuals are weighted by $w_i = \sqrt{\max(c_i, 0.05)}$.

\subsection{Finding the contact and initialising}

A contact is the split of the track into two arcs, each fitted by a quadratic in $t$ per pixel
coordinate, that at least halves the residual of one quadratic over the whole track. The contact pixel,
where the arcs meet, back-projects to $(x_b, y_b, R)$; four further anchor candidates are tried, the
velocities on each side come from the linear solves above, and the best refined fit is kept (Section~S0).

\subsection{Joint refinement}

The delivery is parameterised by $\boldsymbol{\theta} = (t_b, x_b, y_b,\allowbreak v_x^-, v_y^-, v_z^-,\allowbreak v_x^+, v_y^+, e)$,
with $v_z^+ = -e\, v_z^-$, so
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}(t) = (x_b, y_b, R) + \mathbf{v}^{\pm} \tau - \tfrac{1}{2} g \tau^2 \hat{\mathbf{z}}, \quad \tau = t - t_b,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
taking $\mathbf{v}^-$ for $\tau < 0$ and $\mathbf{v}^+$ for $\tau \geq 0$. The residual vector stacks the
weighted pixel residuals $w_i (u_i - \hat{u}_i)$ and $w_i (v_i - \hat{v}_i)$ of every frame with three soft
priors: $(e - 0.55)/0.15$, $(v_x^+ - v_x^-)/4$ and $(v_y^+ - v_y^-)/2$, in units chosen so that each prior
is worth about one pixel of disagreement. The free post-contact horizontal velocity measures deviation and friction
loss; on a short post-contact arc the priors keep the problem determined. The cost is minimised with a soft-L1 loss at a scale of 2~px by SciPy's bounded trust-region reflective
method~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}, with $t_b$ bounded to the track's span, $e$ to
$[0.25, 0.85]$, and velocities to physical ranges. The pixel radii are not used.

The covariance of the estimate is the linearised $(J^\top \Sigma^{-1} J)^{-1}$, with $J$ the Jacobian of the
unweighted-loss residuals at the solution and $\Sigma$ diagonal: $\hat{\sigma}^2$ for the pixel rows, the
residual variance per degree of freedom of the pixel residuals alone, and unit variance for the three prior
rows, whose spreads are already in their units~\cite{seber1989nonlinear}; when $\hat{\sigma}$ is not
1~px the prior rows are left unscaled. It
is a first-order approximation and Section~\ref{sec:coverage} measures how far it can be trusted.

A full toss, or a clip cut before the pitch, has no split; the single parabola is then solved
linearly~\cite{ribnick2009projectiles}, refined with the apparent-size residual and flagged.

\subsection{Prediction and decision}
\label{sec:decision}

The post-contact parabola is continued to the stump plane $x = 0$; if it would touch down again first, a
further bounce with the same $e$ is applied. The crossing $(y^\star, z^\star)$ and its covariance follow
from~\eqref{eq:model} and a numerical Jacobian of the crossing with respect to $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, giving
$\sigma_y$ and $\sigma_z$. The impact point, in the app and in every experiment, is the model position at
the last tracked frame.

Law 36~\cite{mcc2017laws} is applied as three tests. The ball must not have pitched outside the line of the
leg stump; when no bounce is seen this test is not applied. It must have struck the batter in line with the
stumps (we cannot see whether a shot was offered, so impact outside either line counts as not in line); and
it must be projected to hit the stumps, meaning $|y^\star| \leq w_o$ and $z^\star \leq h_b$ for the centre.
The playing conditions~\cite{icc2023drs} return umpire's call on impact and wickets when the centre is
outside the limit by less than one ball radius $R$, and have no umpire's call on pitching. We add a guard
band~\cite{jcgm106,carullo2024conformity}: a test is passed only if the centre is at least $\kappa\sigma$
inside its limit, and failed only if it is more than $R + \kappa\sigma$ outside ($\kappa\sigma$ with no
radius on the pitching line); anything in between is umpire's call. The guard band therefore adds an
umpire's call on pitching that the playing conditions do not have. In the app $\sigma = w_{95}/1.96$, with
$w_{95}$ the calibrated 95\,\% half-width of that quantity under the rule for the clip's camera end
(Section~\ref{sec:coverage}), and $\kappa = \RiskCalKFive$ (Section~\ref{sec:kcurve}), a value chosen in
sample. Experiment~1 scores the uncalibrated estimator, $\sigma$ from the fit's own covariance and
$\kappa = 1$; Experiments~2 and~3 run the app. In Experiment~1 a fit that fails (it stops on a parameter
bound, has too few detections, or sees a bounce that no anchored fit holds) or gives no stump crossing
returns no verdict, which counts as a disagreement; so does the unanchored baseline's
final fit, after its fallback below. No residual limit is applied. In the app a fit with no stump
crossing returns umpire's call if the pitching and impact tests pass, and not out otherwise.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Experiment 1: synthetic deliveries with exact truth}

A generator integrates each delivery at 1~kHz with physics richer than the estimator's: drag with $C_d =
0.5$ on a 0.16~kg ball, swing~\cite{mehta2005swing} of $-2$ to 2~m\,s$^{-2}$ before contact, a friction loss
of 5 to 20\,\% and a lateral kick of $-2$ to 2~m/s at contact, and restitution from 0.40 to 0.70. Release speed is drawn from 60 to 150~km/h, the aimed
pitching length (the drag-free target; drag moves the true contact further from the stumps) from 1 to
10~m, line from $-0.6$ to $0.6$~m, release height from 1.8 to 2.3~m. The batter's pad intercepts the ball at
the popping crease, 1.22~m from the stumps, so detections stop there and the estimator must predict the rest; the
true crossing is the integrated trajectory continued untouched, and the true verdict follows from the true
contact, crease and stump-plane points by the same Law 36 tests with $\kappa = 0$. The estimated impact is
read at the last tracked frame, at or before the crease.

Observations are the projections of the true positions at the frame rate, with Gaussian pixel noise on the
centre, 10\% multiplicative noise on the radius, a dropout probability per frame, and the calibration taps
perturbed by Gaussian noise before the pose is re-solved, so the estimator never receives the true camera; the focal length is solved from the taps with $L$ fixed.
The real detector's centre error has not been measured, so we do not know which noise row a real clip sits in.
The reference configuration is 60~Hz, 1~px centre noise, 5\% dropout, 2~px tap noise, all physical effects
on, and an upright phone 2.8~m behind the striker's stumps at 1.7~m height. Each factor is swept in turn over
the same \GeoBalls{} random deliveries, so rows differ only in that factor, and six camera placements are
tested at the reference noise: three positions at the striker's end, a lateral offset of 1.5~m, and two
placements at the bowler's end (Table~S2 of the supplement); frame timing is tested separately
(Table~S1). Counting the reference setting once, this is \GeoSettings{} distinct settings and \GeoRuns{}
runs. At the reference setting \RefNotOutBalls{} of the \GeoBalls{} balls are truly not out,
\RefOutBalls{} out and \RefUmpBalls{} umpire's call, so always saying not out agrees
\RefAlwaysNotOutPct\% of the time; the true verdict, and so this trivial rate, moves with the
physics, and pooled over all runs it is \GeoAlwaysNotOutPct\%. Pooled counts are over runs; the runs of
one ball are not independent, so confidence bounds are taken over balls.

\textbf{World~B.} The uncertainty experiments use a second set of \CovBN{} deliveries, drawn the same way,
half filmed from the reference camera and half from the same placement behind the bowler's stumps, half
of each to a left-handed batter, with lateral quantities signed to the leg side. $C_d$ is drawn from 0.35
to 0.55 and every input of
the uncertainty budget (Table~S4) drawn from its probability density: tap noise, frame-time jitter and frame-rate
metadata, rolling-shutter readout, lens distortion, and pitch, stump and bail dimensions.
Two baselines get identical detections. The unanchored one uses the same split but fits a free
six-parameter parabola with the apparent-size cue to each side, so the anchor is the only thing it lacks;
without a usable split it falls back to a single parabola over the whole track, the second baseline.

\subsection{Experiment 2: rendered clips through the full pipeline}

To include detection and association error, the fixed 100-delivery grid of five speeds (90 to 145~km/h),
five lines ($0, \pm 0.2, \pm 0.4$~m) and four lengths (2, 4, 6 and 8~m) is rendered by our own renderer to 1080 by 1920 portrait
clips at 60~Hz with a flat pitch, stumps, creases and a motion-smeared ball, and each clip is submitted to the
production job with the calibration marks perturbed by \EteTapPx~px, from the reference camera and again from the bowler's end. This tests
the geometry end to end, not the detector's robustness to clutter.

\subsection{Experiment 3: real clips}

Three phone clips of net deliveries are analysed (Table~\ref{tab:real}). Their frames show one outdoor
net lane from one fixed viewpoint, titled off-spin, leg-spin and off-spin.
For all three the solved pose puts the camera behind the bowler's stumps, so the bowler's-end rule applies.
The clips are 1080 by 1920 portrait H.264 at a nominal 60~Hz, and each decoded frame's own timestamp is
used. No metric truth exists for these clips; we report the output with its stated uncertainty, half-rate
consistency and independent checks. Each clip is analysed on all sampled frames, on the even frames only
and on the odd frames only. The halves share calibration and detector, so their spread measures
only frame-level noise, not repeatability in the sense of the VIM.
All revision numbers come from a pinned software environment. In it test5 tracks fewer points than in
the submitted version; the submitted environment cannot be rebuilt, so we could not trace the cause.

Agreement is the share of deliveries returned in their true class; a false out is a not-out returned as
out, a missed out the reverse. Errors are absolute differences, reported as medians and 90th percentiles.

Each input of Table~S4 (supplement), Type A or Type B, was propagated through the calibration solve and
the anchored fit following JCGM~100~\cite{jcgm100}, all inputs taken as uncorrelated, although the pitch,
stump and bail dimensions enter one pose solve. The measurement groups alone give \BudRepMeasY{} and
\BudRepMeasZ~mm on a representative delivery, against a pipeline sigma of \BudRepPipeSigmaY{} and
\BudRepPipeSigmaZ~mm; the covariance leaves out the flight model, whose largest input is swing. A Monte
Carlo propagation in the manner of JCGM~101~\cite{jcgm101} ($M = \McM$) gives a lateral standard deviation
of \McUY~mm against the linear \McUcY~mm and a vertical 95\,\% interval of $[\McLowZ, \McHighZ]$~mm against
the linear $[\McGumLowZ, \McGumHighZ]$~mm, because swing of either sign leaves the fit low. It did not reach
the JCGM~101 tolerance (twice the batch standard deviation \McTwoSdBatch~mm against \McTol~mm) and is stable
only at one significant digit (Table~S5), but its gaps to the linear budget, up to \McDHighZ~mm, are far
larger, so the linear budget serves only to rank the inputs.

Table~\ref{tab:coverage} tests the stated sigma against the simulated error $\varepsilon$. Rolling shutter is
not the cause of the fit-only shortfall (Table~S6). The missing term is model error $\sigma_\mathrm{mod}$, fitted per
camera end and quantity by maximum likelihood as a function of the bounce-to-stump distance $x_b$, with its
form (constant, proportional to $x_b$, or the two in quadrature) chosen by held-out likelihood. In the
app's rule the term is \CalStrikerYForm{} in $y$ and \CalStrikerZForm{} in $z$ at the striker's end, and
\CalBowlerYForm{} in $y$ and \CalBowlerZForm{} in $z$ at the bowler's end (Table~S10). Swing, the largest
flight-model input, explains \CalStrikerYSwingRsq\% and \CalBowlerYSwingRsq\% of the lateral error's
variance at the two ends; the model term absorbs it and no swing parameter is fitted. With the model term
and before any coverage factor, one-sigma coverage is \CovBFullYOne\% in $y$ and \CovBFullZOne\% in $z$
against a nominal 68.3\,\%, so the height now over-covers.

Two further steps are each fitted on four folds and tested on the fifth. First, for each end and quantity,
no bias is compared with a bias line $b = a + c\,x_b$ by held-out likelihood. The line wins for height and for
the pitching point at both ends; no lateral or impact bias is applied. For height it is chosen in
\CalBowlerZBiasFolds{} of five folds at the bowler's end but in only \CalStrikerZBiasFolds{} at the striker's.
The bowler's-end height line, $a = \CalBowlerZBiasA \pm \CalBowlerZBiasUA$~mm
and $c = \CalBowlerZBiasC \pm \CalBowlerZBiasUC$~mm/m, is subtracted and its standard uncertainty $u_b$,
median \CalBowlerZBiasMedU~mm, is added to $\sigma$; it moves the mean height error from
\CalBowlerZMeanBefore{} to \CalBowlerZMeanAfter~mm. Its evidence is the held-out selection alone; we make no
claim of significance. Coverage here selects the bias inside each training fold; the shipped rule takes the
higher held-out likelihood summed over all five. Second, split-conformal coverage factors~\cite{vovk2005alrw,angelopoulos2023gentle},
the 68.3 and 95\,\% quantiles of $|\varepsilon|/\sigma$ on the training folds, are set per end and per third
of $x_b$. $k_{95}$ spans \CalStrikerYKNinetyfive{} in $y$ and \CalStrikerZKNinetyfive{} in $z$ at the
striker's end and \CalBowlerYKNinetyfive{} and \CalBowlerZKNinetyfive{} at the bowler's end. Only
deliveries with a bounce fit enter: \CalStrikerYN{} at the striker's end and \CalBowlerYN{} at the bowler's
(\CalStrikerExcluded{} and \CalBowlerExcluded{} excluded, of which \DenStrikerNotFitted{} and \DenBowlerNotFitted{} failed fits). Held out, pooled over both ends, the half-widths
cover \CalYCovSixtyeight\% [\CalYCovSixtyeightLo, \CalYCovSixtyeightHi] and
\CalYCovNinetyfive\% [\CalYCovNinetyfiveLo, \CalYCovNinetyfiveHi] in $y$ and \CalZCovSixtyeight\%
[\CalZCovSixtyeightLo, \CalZCovSixtyeightHi] and \CalZCovNinetyfive\% [\CalZCovNinetyfiveLo,
\CalZCovNinetyfiveHi] in $z$ (binomial 95\,\% intervals); by end and third see Table~S10. Split-conformal
factors give coverage near nominal by construction when calibration and test errors are exchangeable, so
these figures check the implementation and the calibration set, not the simulator's physics. A fit that saw no
bounce is outside what the rule was fitted on: it gets no bias correction, the largest model term and the widest factor of its end.

The instrument therefore outputs the crossing $(y, z)$ with half-widths $w_{68} = k_{68}\sigma$ and
$w_{95} = k_{95}\sigma$, where $\sigma^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{fit}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{cal}^2 + u_b^2 +
\sigma_\mathrm{mod}^2$. These are half-widths of intervals calibrated on simulated errors, not the
standard and expanded uncertainties $u$ and $U_{95}$ of JCGM~100. Median $w_{95}$ is
\CalStrikerYMedUNinetyfive{} and \CalStrikerZMedUNinetyfive~mm at the striker's end and
\CalBowlerYMedUNinetyfive{} and \CalBowlerZMedUNinetyfive~mm at the bowler's end. The constants hold under
the simulator's physics; Table~\ref{tab:real} applies the bowler's-end rule to the real clips with
$\sigma_\mathrm{cal}$ from each clip's own taps.

\subsection{Choosing the guard band from false-out risk}
\label{sec:kcurve}

Following JCGM~106~\cite{jcgm106}, the specific risk of an OUT call is the probability, given the estimate,
that the true verdict is not out, $1 - \prod_i \Phi((m_i + R_i)/\sigma_i)$ over the signed margins $m_i$
of the four limits (pitching line, impact line, stump edge, top of the bails), with $R_i = R$ except on the
pitching line. Two approximations enter: the product treats the four tests as independent, although the
impact-line and stump-edge positions share fit parameters, and the prior is flat, which ignores the base
rate of not-outs. Fig.~S1 of the supplement traces the risk on the \RiskN{} World~B deliveries with a
verdict, \RiskTruthOut{} of them truly out, with each end's rule applied and $\sigma_i = w_{95}/1.96$ from
the held-out calibration. That $\sigma_i$ assumes normal errors; the intervals are calibrated, but the
held-out one-sided tail beyond $1.645\sigma_i$ runs from \CalExceedMin\% to \CalExceedMax\% across ends and
quantities against the 5\,\% a normal law gives (Section~S11). The former rule, fit sigma and $\kappa = 1$,
makes out calls whose modelled risk reaches \RiskFormerMaxSpecCal\%, with \RiskFormerOutsOverFiveCal{}
above 5\,\%. The app's $\kappa = \RiskCalKFive$ is the smallest factor that keeps every out call on these
deliveries at or below 5\,\% (largest \RiskShippedMaxSpecCal\%), and $\kappa = \RiskCalKOne$ keeps them
below 1\,\%. Both are chosen in sample, on the deliveries the risk is reported on, so they do not bound the
risk of a new delivery. The factors that bound the modelled risk for any delivery, under independent normal
tests with this $\sigma_i$ and all four limits binding at once, are \RiskBoundFourFive{} and
\RiskBoundFourOne; with the tails above, even they do not bound the true risk.

The rule is for avoiding false outs, and that is what it does on these deliveries: \RiskCalFalseOutFive{}
false outs and \RiskCalMissedOutFive{} missed outs, with \RiskCalOutRecallFive\% of true outs called out and
\RiskCalOutToUmpFive\% returned as umpire's call. Its agreement, \RiskCalAgreeFive\% (\RiskFitKOneAgree\%
under the former rule), is below the \RiskAlwaysNotOutPct\% of always saying not out.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{No independent reference.} Every accuracy number and the calibration come from our own simulator
and renderer. Rendered from the bowler's end, the app agrees on \EteBowlerAgree{} of \EteBowlerN{} clips with
\EteBowlerNoVerdict{} no verdict, and it declines \RealDeclinedRuns{} of the \RealRuns{} real-clip analyses. The size
check finds the scale off on the one real clip that allows it; the gravity check is too noisy to decide. Two ran with the pitch length free, which no simulation tests, and fitted
a few metres; only \RealClipsUsed{} of \RealClipsFound{} candidate clips could be calibrated. Time comes from the phone's clock, unchecked. Repeatability over repeated deliveries and
reproducibility across phones, operators and calibrations are not measured.

\textbf{One baseline family.} Both baselines share our detections and split; a filter-based
tracker~\cite{ghimire2026cricket} was not reimplemented.

\textbf{The calibration is the simulator's.} A real pitch, ball or phone may need a different bias line,
model term and coverage factors; on rendered clips the lateral half-width is too wide and the height
half-width too narrow at 2~m. The guard factor is chosen in sample, and the risk bound assumes normal tails
the held-out errors do not have. An ambiguous contact split makes the solution multimodal.

\textbf{The estimator ignores drag, swing, friction and spin.} Swing with a side-force coefficient of 0.3
moves the median lateral error from \PhysBaselineMedY{} to \PhysSwingCsZeroThreeMedY~mm and biases the
height by \PhysSwingCsZeroThreeMeanZ~mm; a pitch with restitution 0.40 biases it by
\PhysRestitutionZeroFourZeroMeanZ~mm (Table~S7).

\textbf{The contact must be seen, and calibration is manual.} A full toss, or a hidden contact, falls back
to the flagged single-parabola fit. With no bounce seen the pitching test is not applied, which favours
out when a bounce outside leg was missed. Twelve taps are required; detecting the marks would remove them.

\section{Conclusion}

In simulation, a single phone behind the striker's stumps can reconstruct a cricket delivery in metres if
it sees the bounce, which fixes a point on a known plane at a known instant; from the bowler's end it agrees
less often than always saying not out. The rule the app ships is built to
avoid false outs: on \RiskN{} simulated deliveries it makes \RiskCalFalseOutFive{} false outs, calls
\RiskCalOutRecallFive\% of true outs out and returns \RiskCalOutToUmpFive\% as umpire's call, and its
agreement, \RiskCalAgreeFive\%, is below the \RiskAlwaysNotOutPct\% of always saying not out. Its guard
factor is chosen in sample. The uncalibrated estimator agrees in \GeoAgreeAll\% of runs against a trivial
\GeoAlwaysNotOutPct\%, with median stump-plane errors of \GeoMedYAll{} and \GeoMedZAll~mm. On rendered clips from the striker's end the lateral half-width is too wide and the
height half-width covers \EteCoverZLenTwo{} of \EteCoverZLenTwoN{} balls pitched at 2~m. The real clips ran
on an untested calibration mode that gave implausible scale, and no real delivery has been measured with a
reference.
